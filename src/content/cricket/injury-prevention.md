## Who this is for, and how it differs from the plyometrics page

Healthy adults (18 and over) who play recreational or club cricket and have been cleared to exercise. It is education, not diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation or return to play. It is not for under-18s, who need qualified supervision (see the [youth consensus claim](/strength/cricket/#claim-youth-training-consensus)), nor for anyone with a current or recent injury, a recent operation, pregnancy or the first months after birth, or a heart, lung, joint or nerve condition.

The [plyometrics injury-prevention page](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/injury-prevention/) lists what is commonly injured, the general prevention claims, a referral table and the pain rules. This page does not repeat them. It adds the hamstring, shoulder and back evidence that sits on the strength side.

## What the evidence says, by area

Nearly all of it is professional, academy or young players. Rates for adult club cricketers were not found. The population is stated on each claim in the evidence list at the foot of the page.

**Across the body**

- In elite men's domestic cricket in England and Wales, the thigh was the most commonly injured area and lumbar spine injuries were the most prevalent, with 7.5% of players unavailable on any given day ([claim](#claim-cricket-domestic-injuries), grade C). It is professional and cannot be applied to club cricket.
- Across other sports, strength training programmes were associated with fewer sports injuries in trials of people aged 12 to 40 ([claim](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-strength-training-injuries), grade A). It does not show what a cricket programme should contain.

**Hamstring**

- Fast bowlers carried the highest hamstring injury risk among roles in Australian professionals, and previous hamstring injury was a risk factor ([claim](#claim-cricket-hamstring-risk-factors), grade C).
- The Nordic hamstring exercise, within injury prevention programmes, was associated with fewer hamstring injuries in several sports, mostly football ([claim](#claim-nordic-hamstring-injury), grade A). No cricket trial was found.
- In 74 male cricketers at three levels, eccentric hamstring strength on that exercise did not differ by level or role ([claim](#claim-cricket-eccentric-hamstring-levels), grade C). So a single strength number does not separate players, and no cricket threshold exists.
- UK professional clubs used the exercise but rarely ran the full programme, and named players' views and culture as barriers ([survey](#claim-cricket-nordic-uptake), grade D).

**Shoulder**

- In 105 elite South African cricketers, 17% had a non-contact dominant-shoulder injury in a season, and two screening findings that were not strength measures (a thickened supraspinatus tendon and a shortened pectoralis minor on the other side) were risk factors ([claim](#claim-cricket-shoulder-screening), grade C).
- In 26 elite Australian female fast bowlers, shoulder rotation strength did not differ between those with and without a history of shoulder pain ([claim](#claim-cricket-shoulder-strength-female), grade C).
- A review of shoulder injury prevention programmes in overhead athletes could not conclude that they work ([claim](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-shoulder-programmes), contested), so the exercises below are conventions, not proven protection.

**Back**

- Back injuries and stress fractures are common in young and professional fast bowlers ([claim](#claim-cricket-back-injuries), grade C). Reviews found the risk-factor evidence inconsistent and the intervention evidence too weak to conclude ([2023 review](#claim-cricket-lbp-intrinsic-review), contested; [earlier review](#claim-cricket-lbp-risk-interventions), grade C). So no trunk exercise is shown to protect a bowler's back. In professional bowlers, trunk side strain was not linked with side-bridge endurance ([claim](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-side-strain)).

**Workload**

- The workload pattern that went with injury differed by tissue in one 15-year cohort of fast bowlers, and tendon injuries followed sudden increases ([claim](#claim-cricket-workload-tissue-types), grade C). The ratios used to monitor load are contested ([claim](#claim-acwr-contested)). More on the [fast bowlers page](/strength/cricket/fast-bowlers/). No number from these studies is a limit for you.

## What exists on this site, by area (conventions)

These are existing exercises, linked, not copied. Each page has its own contraindications, regression and pain rule. They are general training, not rehabilitation, and none is shown to prevent a cricket injury.

- **Hamstring:** [Nordic hamstring curl](/strength/exercises/nordic-curl/) (advanced, optional, introduce gradually) and [seated leg curl](/strength/exercises/seated-leg-curl/).
- **Groin:** [adductor machine](/strength/exercises/adductor-machine/). Groin injuries were among the common first-class injuries ([claim](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-injury-sites)). No cricket groin-prevention trial was found.
- **Shoulder:** [face pull](/strength/exercises/face-pull/) and [cable external rotation](/strength/exercises/cable-external-rotation/), light and pain-free. Evidence limited and mixed.
- **Trunk:** [side plank](/strength/exercises/side-plank/), [Pallof press](/strength/exercises/pallof-press/) and [bird dog](/strength/exercises/bird-dog/), after the [back-safe core guide](/core/) if your back is not settled. No cricket evidence that they prevent back injury.

Load build-up, landing, hops and stopping work are on the [plyometrics cricket pages](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/). A convention that applies to both: build up gradually after a break, and avoid a sudden jump in bowling, throwing, sprinting or training in a single week. No source gives a safe size for that jump.

## What is not known

- No trial of an exercise programme with injury outcomes in adult club cricketers.
- No cricket trial of the Nordic exercise, of shoulder strengthening, or of any trunk exercise.
- Nothing on injury in spin bowlers, wicketkeepers or masters players. The searches were not exhaustive.
- Not published: a pre-season hamstring strength-testing meta-analysis (a correction notice exists and was not read), and a workload paper with implausibly wide intervals. Read and not needed: a pre-season screening study in professionals that found only weak predictors. Every source was read by abstract only. No full text was read.

## Red flags

Back pain that persists or keeps returning, especially in a bowler, needs a clinician. Back pain with leg weakness, numbness around the saddle area or new bladder or bowel changes is urgent. A sudden pop, snap or tearing sensation in the thigh, the side of the trunk or the shoulder needs a clinician before you train or play again. Pain that has not improved after two to three weeks, or that returns every time a movement is repeated, needs a physiotherapist. The full tiers are on the [plyometrics injury-prevention page](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/injury-prevention/#red-flags). This page does not diagnose, rehabilitate or decide return to play.
