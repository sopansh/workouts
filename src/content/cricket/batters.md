## Who this is for

Healthy adults (18 and over) who bat at recreational or club level and have been cleared to exercise. This page is education and a selection of existing exercises. It is not a programme shown to work for batters.

It is not for under-18s, who need qualified supervision (see the [youth consensus claim](/strength/cricket/#claim-youth-training-consensus) on the overview page). It is not for anyone with a current or recent injury, back pain that has not been assessed, a recent operation, pregnancy or the first months after birth, or a heart, lung, joint or nerve condition. Elite and professional batters should work with a qualified coach.

## What the evidence says

Batting has less research than bowling. Everything found is in elite or professional players, and the study sizes are small. The population is stated on each claim in the evidence list at the foot of the page.

- **Demands are poorly measured.** A brief review says the physical demands of batting have been neglected and that few studies exist ([claim](#claim-cricket-batting-demands-review), grade D). So the demands listed above are hypotheses.
- **Upper-body strength and hitting.** In 18 provincial-level elite batsmen, bench-press strength went with the maximum distance hit in a batting task but not with batting average or strike rate in matches ([claim](#claim-cricket-batting-upper-body-strength), grade C). It is a correlation. It does not show that a stronger bench press makes a batter hit further, and it does not link strength to scoring.
- **Running between the wickets.** In 20 batters in a simulated innings, twelve sprints an over lowered knee extensor and flexor strength and slowed sprint times from the third over, and six sprints an over did much less ([claim](#claim-cricket-batting-repeated-shuttles), grade C). It is a short acute study of fatigue, not a training study, and it measured no injuries.
- **Jumping and role.** In 19 international men, batters scored higher than bowlers on two jump tests, and other measures differed little ([claim](#claim-cricket-profiles-bowlers-batters), grade C). It describes one small group and links no quality to performance.
- **Injuries.** In elite men's domestic cricket in England and Wales, batting caused fewer match injuries per day of play than bowling or fielding ([claim](#claim-cricket-domestic-injuries), grade C). Batting-onset hamstring injuries were particularly common in one-day matches in Australian professionals ([claim](#claim-cricket-hamstring-risk-factors), grade C). In community cricket, being struck by the ball was the most common cause of hospital-treated injury across roles and ages ([community injuries](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-community-injuries), grade C), and batting accounted for a wide range of injuries, 7% to 49%, in studies that included all activities ([claim](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-community-evidence), grade C). Use properly fitted protective equipment suited to your role. The reviews did not test which equipment works.
- **Power and rotation.** No study of rotational power or medicine-ball training in batters was found. The nearest tested example is in women handball players ([claim](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-medicine-ball-trial), grade B), and a meta-analysis found that lower-body plyometrics in healthy adults was associated with small-to-moderate gains in jump, sprint and strength tests ([claim](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-plyometric-adults), grade A). Neither tested batting.

## A short strength block (a convention)

This is a selection of exercises already on this site, listed with the reason and the state of the evidence for each. The selection is a coaching convention. It is not a study result, and no source shows that it improves batting or lowers injury. Doses, loads and progression come from each exercise's page and from the [volume](/strength/principles/volume/) and [progression](/strength/principles/progression/) principles. This page sets none.

A convention for frequency: up to two short gym sessions a week, four to six exercises from this list in each. Season placement is on the [season planning page](/strength/cricket/season-planning/).

- **[Goblet squat](/strength/exercises/goblet-squat/) and [Bulgarian split squat](/strength/exercises/bulgarian-split-squat/).** Leg strength for sprints, turns and the stance. Evidence: a hypothesis. The only sourced link is that repeated sprints tired the knee extensors.
- **[Barbell hip thrust](/strength/exercises/hip-thrust/).** Hip extension strength for sprinting, with a low spinal-load rating on its page. Evidence: a hypothesis.
- **[Seated leg curl](/strength/exercises/seated-leg-curl/).** Knee-flexor work on a machine. Repeated shuttles lowered knee-flexor strength, and hamstring injuries occur in batting. Evidence: no trial of any hamstring exercise in batters.
- **[Dumbbell bench press](/strength/exercises/dumbbell-bench-press/).** Upper-body pressing. Evidence: the bench-press correlation with hitting distance only, which is not a training effect.
- **[Chest-supported row](/strength/exercises/chest-supported-row/).** A pulling exercise to balance the pressing. Evidence: a convention.
- **[Pallof press](/strength/exercises/pallof-press/) and [Side plank](/strength/exercises/side-plank/).** Trunk work that resists rotation and side-flexion. Evidence: none in batters. A meta-analysis found core training changes trunk-strength tests more than athletic performance ([claim](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/evidence/#claim-plyo-cricket-core-limits), grade A). If your back is not settled, use the [back-safe core guide](/core/) first.

Read each exercise page for its contraindications, regression and pain rule before using it. Some need a gym.

## What is not known

- No training study in batters was found, at any level.
- Nothing was found on batting rotation, the stance, or running between the wickets in club players. The searches were not exhaustive.
- The two strength studies are correlations or acute fatigue in small elite groups. They do not show that gym work improves batting.
- Read but not used: a 2024 profile of women's sub-elite cricketers by role. Every source was read by abstract only. No full text was read.

## Red flags

Stop and see a clinician for a sudden pop, pull or tearing in the back of the thigh while running between the wickets, for back pain that persists or keeps returning, and for any blow from the ball to the head or face that causes confusion, vomiting, a worsening headache or drowsiness (urgent), or a blow to the eye with pain or any change in vision (urgent). A finger or hand struck by the ball that looks deformed, cannot be moved or swells quickly also needs a clinician. The full list is on the [plyometrics injury-prevention page](/plyometrics/sports/cricket/injury-prevention/#red-flags). This page does not diagnose, rehabilitate or decide return to play.
